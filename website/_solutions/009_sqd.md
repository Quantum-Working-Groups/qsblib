## Algorithm Overview

Sample-Based Quantum Diagonalization (SQD) is a hybrid quantum-classical eigensolver that approximates correlated electronic ground states using quantum-generated configuration samples and classical Hamiltonian diagonalization. Rather than requiring a quantum processor to estimate the molecular energy through repeated measurements of all Hamiltonian terms, SQD uses the processor primarily to generate occupation-number bitstrings. These samples identify candidate Slater determinants for reduced electronic configuration spaces, in which the Hamiltonian matrix elements and approximate eigenvectors are computed classically.

The motivation is the combinatorial growth of the full configuration interaction (FCI) space. Although exact diagonalization supplies a reference solution within a chosen finite orbital basis, it becomes prohibitively expensive as the number of electrons and orbitals increases. SQD instead assumes that a useful approximation to the target eigenstate can be obtained from a comparatively small collection of important configurations. Quantum state preparation supplies information about which configurations to consider, while a classical eigensolver determines their optimal amplitudes within each selected subspace. The circuit-generated state need not be an accurate representation of the final eigenvector in every detail, provided its measurements reveal sufficient support for a high-quality selected-space approximation.

The original method combines computational-basis sampling with **self-consistent configuration recovery**. Hardware errors can produce measured bitstrings that violate known electron-number constraints. Rather than discarding all such samples, the recovery procedure uses occupation estimates derived from the current classical subspace eigenvectors to probabilistically correct the configurations. The recovered sample distribution is used to construct new determinant batches, which are diagonalized again. This iterative exchange between configuration recovery and classical eigensolution improves the usefulness of a fixed quantum measurement dataset without requiring a new quantum optimization loop after every classical update.

SQD is particularly relevant to molecular dissociation, open-shell and multireference chemistry, transition-metal complexes, and large active spaces whose correlated states are compressible in a suitable determinant basis. Its efficiency is not determined solely by the number of qubits or the size of the quantum circuit. It also depends on the probability of sampling physically important configurations, the quality of recovery, the sizes of the selected subspaces, and the cost of classical eigensolvers. If the ground state requires an exponentially large set of important determinants in the chosen orbital basis, these classical and sampling requirements may remain prohibitive.

The associated Problem is **Electronic Structure of Large and Highly Correlated Molecular Systems**, for which the primary targets are the electronic ground-state energy and wavefunction. The foundational SQD study provides concrete demonstrations for nitrogen dissociation and synthetic iron-sulfur clusters. Those results establish useful methodological capabilities but do not establish a general quantum computational advantage over optimized classical selected-configuration methods. A meaningful comparison must isolate the benefit of quantum configuration sampling and account for the entire quantum-classical workflow.

## Methodology and Execution

### Molecular Hamiltonian and Selected-Determinant Representation

For molecular electronic structure, the nonrelativistic Hamiltonian in a finite spin-orbital basis is

$$
\hat{H}
=
\sum_{pq}h_{pq}a_p^\dagger a_q
+
\frac{1}{2}\sum_{pqrs}g_{pqrs}
 a_p^\dagger a_q^\dagger a_s a_r
+
E_{\mathrm{nuc}},
$$

where $$a_p^\dagger$$ and $$a_p$$ are fermionic creation and annihilation operators, $$h_{pq}$$ and $$g_{pqrs}$$ are the one- and two-electron integrals in the specified convention, and $$E_{\mathrm{nuc}}$$ is the nuclear repulsion energy. The target state satisfies $$\hat{H}\lvert\Psi_0\rangle=E_0\lvert\Psi_0\rangle$$ in the required electron-number and spin sector.

For $$N_e$$ active electrons in $$M$$ spatial orbitals, the fixed-electron-number determinant-space dimension is

$$
D_{\mathrm{FCI}}=\binom{2M}{N_e}.
$$

When the numbers of spin-up and spin-down electrons are fixed separately, the corresponding space has dimension

$$
D_{\mathrm{FCI}}
=
\binom{M}{N_\alpha}\binom{M}{N_\beta},
\qquad N_\alpha+N_\beta=N_e.
$$

In a conventional occupation-number encoding such as Jordan–Wigner, each spin-orbital is associated with one qubit, giving $$N_q=2M$$ qubits before symmetry reductions or additional hardware requirements. A computational-basis state $$\lvert\mathbf{x}\rangle$$ represents a fermionic occupation pattern $$\mathbf{x}=(x_1,\ldots,x_{N_q})$$ with $$x_p\in\{0,1\}$$. Its total electron number is $$N(\mathbf{x})=\sum_p x_p$$, and the spin-resolved counts are obtained by summing over the corresponding spin-orbital indices.

SQD represents a fragment or molecular approximate eigenstate as a linear combination of a selected determinant set $$\mathcal{S}$$,

$$
\lvert\Psi_{\mathcal{S}}\rangle
=
\sum_{\mathbf{x}\in\mathcal{S}}
 c_{\mathbf{x}}\lvert\mathbf{x}\rangle.
$$

The distinction between sampling and diagonalization is essential: quantum measurements identify candidate determinants, but the coefficients $$c_{\mathbf{x}}$$ are determined by the classical projected Hamiltonian eigenproblem, not by assigning the square roots of the sampled probabilities as wavefunction amplitudes. The resulting state can therefore differ significantly from the circuit state that supplied its configurations.

### Quantum State Preparation and Computational-Basis Sampling

A quantum circuit prepares an approximate correlated electronic state,

$$
\lvert\Psi_{\mathrm{prep}}(\boldsymbol{\theta})\rangle
=
U(\boldsymbol{\theta})\lvert\Phi_{\mathrm{ref}}\rangle,
$$

where $$\lvert\Phi_{\mathrm{ref}}\rangle$$ is a chosen reference state and $$U(\boldsymbol{\theta})$$ is a state-preparation circuit. Hartree–Fock and other classically constructed approximate states are possible references. The preparation circuit may be parameterized or otherwise designed to populate configurations important to the correlated target state, but SQD does not inherently require end-to-end variational optimization of the circuit on quantum hardware.

The original experimental work uses a truncated **local-unitary cluster Jastrow (LUCJ)** ansatz. It combines fermionic orbital rotations with density-density correlations, which can be represented schematically as

$$
\lvert\Psi_{\mathrm{LUCJ}}\rangle
=
U_{\mathrm{orb}}U_{\mathrm{Jastrow}}
U_{\mathrm{orb}}^\dagger\lvert\Phi_{\mathrm{HF}}\rangle,
\qquad
\hat{J}=\sum_{pq}J_{pq}\hat{n}_p\hat{n}_q.
$$

The locality restrictions select a hardware-manageable subset of density-density couplings. In the foundational hardware demonstrations, LUCJ parameters were initialized using classical coupled-cluster information, without a full hardware-in-the-loop VQE optimization. These are implementation choices of that study rather than mandatory ingredients of every SQD calculation.

Repeated measurement in the computational basis samples configurations according to the ideal probability

$$
P(\mathbf{x})
=
\left\lvert
\langle\mathbf{x}\vert\Psi_{\mathrm{prep}}\rangle
\right\rvert^2.
$$

A measurement budget of $$S$$ shots produces a collection of bitstrings, possibly containing duplicates. Their frequencies reflect the prepared state as well as gate errors, decoherence, readout errors, and finite-shot fluctuations. The quantum state does not need to assign the exact target amplitudes to the configurations; it must instead assign adequate sampling weight to a useful subset of determinants. Configurations with very small preparation probabilities may remain undiscovered even if they are significant in the exact ground state.

Nonrelativistic electronic Hamiltonians without spin-dependent terms conserve the spin-resolved electron numbers. The intended occupation patterns therefore obey

$$
N_\alpha(\mathbf{x})=N_\alpha^{\mathrm{target}},
\qquad
N_\beta(\mathbf{x})=N_\beta^{\mathrm{target}}.
$$

These constraints provide a practical means of identifying noise-corrupted samples. They constrain the spin projection but do not, by themselves, guarantee that a selected-space eigenvector is an eigenstate of $$\hat{\mathbf{S}}^2$$. Spin adaptation and explicit spin diagnostics remain important for open-shell molecules and closely spaced spin states.

### Configuration Recovery and Classical Subspace Diagonalization

The measured or recovered configurations are divided into batches, each defining a determinant subspace $$\mathcal{S}^{(k)}$$. For batch $$k$$, the projector and projected Hamiltonian are

$$
\hat{P}_k
=
\sum_{\mathbf{x}\in\mathcal{S}^{(k)}}
\lvert\mathbf{x}\rangle\langle\mathbf{x}\rvert,
\qquad
\hat{H}_k=\hat{P}_k\hat{H}\hat{P}_k.
$$

The matrix elements $$H_{\mathbf{x}\mathbf{y}}=\langle\mathbf{x}\rvert\hat{H}\lvert\mathbf{y}\rangle$$ are constructed classically from the electronic integrals and fermionic excitation rules. An iterative classical eigensolver then finds the lowest eigenvalues and selected-space wavefunctions,

$$
\hat{H}_k\lvert\Psi^{(k)}\rangle
=
E^{(k)}\lvert\Psi^{(k)}\rangle,
\qquad
\lvert\Psi^{(k)}\rangle
=
\sum_{\mathbf{x}\in\mathcal{S}^{(k)}}
 c_{\mathbf{x}}^{(k)}\lvert\mathbf{x}\rangle.
$$

The foundational work uses Davidson-type diagonalization, including distributed classical calculations for large selected spaces. The lowest energy among independently solved batches can be used as the SQD estimate, $$E_{\mathrm{SQD}}=\min_k E^{(k)}$$. Each batch has its own selected subspace, so the energy differences across batches provide a diagnostic of configuration-selection variability rather than a direct statistical confidence interval without an accompanying sampling analysis.

Configuration recovery uses occupations computed from these classical eigenvectors to improve the measured determinant distribution. For instance, the spin-orbital occupation estimate averaged over $$K$$ selected-space solutions is

$$
\overline{n}_p
=
\frac{1}{K}\sum_{k=1}^{K}
\langle\Psi^{(k)}\rvert
 a_p^\dagger a_p
\lvert\Psi^{(k)}\rangle.
$$

For a measured bitstring that violates the target electron counts, the recovery procedure probabilistically changes occupation bits to restore the physical sector. The correction probabilities use the discrepancy between each measured occupation and the current estimate, schematically $$\lvert x_p-\overline{n}_p\rvert$$, together with the required number of corrections. Recovered samples are rebinned into selected spaces, the corresponding Hamiltonians are diagonalized, and the resulting occupations update the next recovery iteration. This cycle can be repeated until energy and occupation diagnostics stabilize.

The recovery operation is a stochastic approximation, not an inverse quantum noise channel and not a guarantee of reconstructing the exact sampling distribution. Correct particle-number configurations can also be corrupted in ways not detected by number constraints. The algorithm's practical performance depends on the information remaining in the raw samples, the recovery prescription, and the classical diagonalization accuracy.

An important operational feature is that **the same raw quantum samples can be reused across multiple classical recovery iterations**. The number of recovery rounds and classical eigensolver calls can thus increase without requiring proportional additional QPU execution. This reduces some quantum measurement requirements relative to repeated quantum energy-estimation loops, while transferring substantial work to classical sampling, Hamiltonian construction, and eigensolution.

### Implementation and Computational Platform

SQD divides the workflow into distinct quantum and classical tasks. The quantum processor prepares approximate electronic states and records computational-basis configurations. Classical resources construct the molecular integrals, process and recover configurations, generate determinant batches, compute sparse projected Hamiltonians, solve the selected-space eigenvalue problems, and analyze convergence. The algorithm is compatible with independent or parallel classical processing of different batches, though aggregate node-hours and elapsed wall-clock time must be distinguished.

The original large-scale experiments use IBM quantum hardware together with the Fugaku supercomputer. Relevant open-source software includes Qiskit for circuit construction, Qiskit SQD for configuration recovery and subspace workflows, PySCF for molecular integrals and classical electronic-structure calculations, and `ffsim` for fermionic state preparation or simulation. The source entry also identifies DICE for distributed selected-configuration calculations. The exact software versions and division of tasks depend on the implementation and should be recorded for each numerical benchmark.

No machine-learning or superintelligence component is required. SQD's configuration selection is based on quantum measurement statistics and classical numerical processing, rather than a trained predictive model.

## Approximation and Error Control

The fundamental approximation in SQD is the restriction of the electronic state to selected determinant spaces. Even when a selected Hamiltonian is diagonalized to numerical precision, it may omit configurations necessary to reproduce the full target wavefunction. The quality of the approximation depends on the sampled state's overlap with important configurations, the orbital basis, the electronic correlation regime, and the number of distinct configurations that can be retained within available classical resources. This representation error must be distinguished from quantum sampling errors, hardware noise, stochastic recovery, and finite classical eigensolver tolerances.

### Variational Properties and Subspace Convergence

For a normalized state in a valid selected determinant subspace and an accurately evaluated, fixed Hermitian Hamiltonian, the Rayleigh–Ritz principle gives

$$
E_{\mathcal{S}}
=
\min_{\lvert\Psi\rangle\in\mathcal{H}_{\mathcal{S}},\,\lVert\Psi\rVert=1}
\langle\Psi\rvert\hat{H}\lvert\Psi\rangle
\geq E_0,
$$

where $$E_0$$ is the exact ground-state energy in the same finite-basis Hilbert space or appropriate symmetry sector. This bound applies to the classical eigenvalue of the specified projected Hamiltonian. It does not quantify the remaining energy error and does not imply the result is an upper bound to a ground-state energy from a different orbital basis or Hamiltonian.

If two selected spaces are nested, $$\mathcal{S}_1\subseteq\mathcal{S}_2$$, their exact lowest projected eigenvalues satisfy $$E_{\mathcal{S}_2}\leq E_{\mathcal{S}_1}$$. SQD normally uses independently sampled and recovered batches that need not be nested, so increasing the shots or changing recovery settings does not imply monotonic energy improvement across separate runs. Convergence should be evaluated over subspace dimensions, shots, recovery iterations, and independent sampling seeds.

### Sampling, Noise, and Spin-Symmetry Errors

For an ideal independent measurement process, the probability that a particular configuration $$\mathbf{x}$$ is observed at least once in $$S$$ shots is

$$
P_{\mathrm{observed}}(\mathbf{x})
=
1-\left[1-P(\mathbf{x})\right]^S.
$$

The expression illustrates why important determinants with very small preparation probabilities are difficult to discover. A favorable energy estimate requires that the collection of sampled and recovered determinants capture the relevant wavefunction support; the observed number of unique raw configurations is bounded by the shot count, but classical recovery can generate additional candidates. The usefulness of those candidates must be established through the resulting physical observables.

Readout errors, gate imperfections, decoherence, and initialization errors alter the observed occupation distribution. Particle-number-aware recovery can mitigate a subset of the resulting errors but does not guarantee restoration of amplitudes or of the ideal distribution. Hardware effects are therefore best assessed by comparing ideal circuit samples, noisy simulations, and hardware measurements at matched classical subspace settings. For open-shell systems, the constraint on $$N_\alpha$$ and $$N_\beta$$ fixes $$S_z$$ but is insufficient to prevent contamination from other total-spin sectors. Spin diagnostics or symmetry-adapted selected spaces should be included when spin-state energies are of interest.

Classical numerical errors arise from incomplete Hamiltonian generation, finite eigensolver residuals, and limited numerical precision. These should be controlled separately from the selected-subspace approximation by tightening eigensolver tolerances and verifying the Hamiltonian construction. The orbital basis and the choice of molecular active space introduce additional physical approximations that cannot be removed solely by increasing the number of quantum measurements.

### State Diagnostics and Validation

Ground-state energy and wavefunction fidelity provide complementary measures of accuracy. With a matched reference energy $$E_{\mathrm{ref}}$$, define $$\Delta E=\lvert E_{\mathrm{SQD}}-E_{\mathrm{ref}}\rvert$$. For small active spaces where a reference FCI wavefunction is available, the squared fidelity is

$$
F
=
\left\lvert
\langle\Psi_{\mathrm{FCI}}\vert\Psi_{\mathrm{SQD}}\rangle
\right\rvert^2.
$$

Near-degenerate systems may require comparison with the corresponding low-energy eigenspace rather than a single arbitrarily chosen eigenvector. The energy variance,

$$
\sigma_H^2
=
\langle\hat{H}^2\rangle
-\langle\hat{H}\rangle^2,
$$

provides a further eigenstate-quality diagnostic and can support extrapolations of approximate energies. A low variance indicates proximity to an eigenstate but does not, by itself, identify that eigenstate as the ground state. The foundational study uses energy-variance analysis for its iron-sulfur examples.

The main approximation sources and appropriate validation procedures are summarized below.

| Error Source | Origin | Validation |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Quantum state preparation | Insufficient useful configuration support | Compare ansätze and reference states |
| Finite sampling | Important determinants missed | Increase shots and repeat sampling |
| Configuration recovery | Inexact occupation-guided corrections | Vary recovery settings and iterations |
| Selected-space truncation | Missing electronic configurations | Increase selected-space dimensions |
| Spin symmetry | Incomplete total-spin sector control | Spin adaptation and spin diagnostics |
| Hardware noise | Gate, readout, and initialization errors | Compare hardware with ideal/noisy simulation |
| Classical eigensolver | Numerical convergence and matrix construction | Tighten residual tolerances and verify integrals |
| Molecular model | Orbital-basis and active-space truncation | Compare larger bases and active spaces |

The variational property of each exact selected-space eigenvalue is mathematically useful, but it is not a general numerical certificate of total molecular accuracy or of an efficient quantum advantage.

## Applied Problems

### Electronic Structure of Large and Highly Correlated Molecular Systems

The associated Problem concerns molecular electronic ground-state energies and many-electron states in regimes where full configuration interaction becomes computationally prohibitive. SQD seeks to replace the complete determinant expansion with quantum-informed selected spaces, and its relevance is determined by energy accuracy, wavefunction fidelity, selected-space size, and complete computational resources. The Problem remains a broad family of molecular models, whereas the following published SQD demonstrations provide concrete validation examples.

### Nitrogen Molecule Dissociation

The foundational *Science Advances* study investigates the dissociation of the nitrogen molecule in the cc-pVDZ basis. Stretching the N–N bond increases the multireference character of the electronic state, making the potential-energy curve an informative test of configuration selection and correlated electronic structure. The reported experiment uses **58 qubits** on IBM quantum hardware and compares the SQD dissociation results with classical heat-bath configuration interaction (HCI) references.

The reported dissociation calculations employ ten batches, ten configuration-recovery iterations, and selected subspaces containing up to **16 million configurations per batch**. Across the reported curve, energy discrepancies from HCI are on the order of tens of milli-Hartree. These calculations demonstrate the combination of quantum-sampled occupation configurations and large-scale classical diagonalization in a physically important molecular problem, but should not be interpreted as uniformly achieving chemical accuracy or establishing a computational advantage over HCI.

### Synthetic Iron-Sulfur Clusters

The study also examines correlated electronic states in two synthetic iron-sulfur clusters. The first, $$[\mathrm{Fe}_2\mathrm{S}_2(\mathrm{SCH}_3)_4]^{2-}$$, is investigated with **45 qubits**. Its low-energy electronic structure reflects strong metal-centered correlation and competing spin configurations. SQD is used to construct approximate low-energy states, which are assessed against HCI and DMRG calculations using energy-variance analysis. These comparisons demonstrate access to multiple electronic states in selected spaces, but accurate spin assignments and excited-state interpretations require independent validation of the relevant symmetry sectors.

The larger cluster, $$[\mathrm{Fe}_4\mathrm{S}_4(\mathrm{SCH}_3)_4]^{2-}$$, is investigated using **77 qubits** on an IBM Heron processor. The reported state-preparation circuits contain up to approximately **10,570 total gates**, including about **3,500 two-qubit gates**. The selected determinant spaces reach approximately $$10^8$$ configurations, and the study uses the Fugaku supercomputer for the large classical diagonalizations. The largest calculations use 100 configuration batches, each distributed over 64 Fugaku nodes; this corresponds to 6,400 node allocations across the batch calculations, not necessarily 6,400 nodes executing simultaneously.

These experiments establish that large-scale classical processing is an essential part of SQD. The qubit counts, circuit gates, determinant dimensions, and supercomputing resources describe specific demonstrations rather than universal SQD resource requirements. The calculations are compared with high-accuracy classical electronic-structure methods, but do not establish a general quantum computational advantage over classical selected-CI algorithms.

### Benchmark Scope and Current Status

The original demonstrations provide evidence for SQD in molecular dissociation and correlated open-shell chemistry, while the associated Problem permits a broader collection of molecular Hamiltonians, geometries, and active spaces. For reproducible benchmarking, each submitted instance must specify the exact Hamiltonian and reference data. Additional target systems, such as transition-metal complexes or other strongly correlated molecules, should not be presented as demonstrated SQD calculations until their results and numerical conditions have been supplied.

The Solution remains marked `published: true`, consistent with the existing YAML and the published methodological studies. Its `validation: "Partially validated"` status reflects the lack of a comprehensive standardized dataset and accuracy-matched end-to-end comparisons across the entire associated Problem family. These metadata fields describe the catalog entry and should not be confused with a claim that SQD has been validated for arbitrary correlated molecular systems.

## Performance Metrics and Scaling

SQD divides resource requirements between quantum circuit execution and classical selected-space processing. The quantum cost is determined by the number and depth of state-preparation circuits, the measurement-shot budget, and the quality of the resulting configuration distribution. Classical resources are required to recover and batch configurations, evaluate electronic Hamiltonian matrix elements, perform iterative subspace diagonalization, and analyze numerical convergence. The relevant performance metric is the total cost of obtaining a specified physical accuracy, not the quantum processor runtime or selected-space dimension in isolation.

### Published Computational Resources

| Demonstrated System | Qubits | Selected-Space / Circuit Detail | Main Validation Target |
| --- | ---: | --- | --- |
| Nitrogen molecule, cc-pVDZ | 58 | Up to 16 million determinants per batch; ten batches and recovery iterations | Dissociation curve against HCI |
| Synthetic two-iron, two-sulfur cluster | 45 | Low-energy states and energy-variance analysis | HCI and DMRG comparisons |
| Synthetic four-iron, four-sulfur cluster | 77 | Up to $$10^8$$ determinants; approximately 10,570 gates, including 3,500 two-qubit gates | Correlated cluster electronic structure |

For the largest iron-sulfur calculation, the published workflow uses 100 batches, each distributed over 64 Fugaku nodes. The scale of this classical stage is significant: the experiment demonstrates the integration of quantum sampling with highly distributed classical diagonalization rather than an eigensolution carried out solely on quantum hardware.

### Configuration-Space and Sampling Requirements

For a selected space containing $$d$$ determinants, a useful compression metric is

$$
f_{\mathrm{subspace}}
=
\frac{d}{D_{\mathrm{FCI}}}.
$$

A small fraction indicates that fewer determinants have been retained than in full CI, but it does not establish that the selected space accurately represents the target state. Energy error, fidelity, and convergence should be reported against $$d$$ as well as against total quantum measurement effort. The number of unique raw configurations cannot exceed the shot count, while recovery may generate additional configurations that require subsequent validation through eigensolution.

For a determinant $$\mathbf{x}$$ with sampling probability $$P(\mathbf{x})$$, its chance of being observed in $$S$$ ideal shots is $$1-[1-P(\mathbf{x})]^S$$. This dependence makes the concentration of the sampling distribution on important configurations central to SQD performance. More shots can improve discovery probability, but the required budget is problem dependent and may be large for delocalized or multireference wavefunctions. The effect of sampling noise should be evaluated through multiple independent seeds and measurement budgets rather than a single selected-space result.

The classical selected-space eigensolver uses sparse Hamiltonian-vector operations of the form

$$
\mathbf{y}=H_{\mathcal{S}}\mathbf{x}.
$$

Dense Hamiltonian storage would generally require $$\mathcal{O}(d^2)$$ memory, which is impractical for the largest reported selected spaces. Practical iterative solvers exploit fermionic Hamiltonian connectivity and distributed matrix-vector multiplication, with costs determined by the number of connected determinants, iteration count, memory layout, and inter-node communication. The selected-space dimension and associated classical runtime may be the dominant scaling limitation even when quantum sampling circuits are relatively shallow.

The recovery loop adds another classical workload: each iteration can require multiple determinant batches, separate eigensolver calls, occupation estimation, and stochastic recovery operations. Reusing the raw quantum measurement dataset can reduce repeated quantum execution but does not remove these classical costs. A useful accounting of total computational work is

$$
W_{\mathrm{SQD}}
=
W_{\mathrm{setup}}
+
W_{\mathrm{QPU}}
+
\sum_{r=1}^{N_{\mathrm{rec}}}
\sum_{k=1}^{K}
W_{\mathrm{diag}}^{(r,k)}
+
W_{\mathrm{recovery}}
+
W_{\mathrm{other}},
$$

where $$N_{\mathrm{rec}}$$ is the number of recovery iterations, $$K$$ is the number of configuration batches, and $$W_{\mathrm{diag}}^{(r,k)}$$ is the work required by batch $$k$$ in recovery iteration $$r$$. This expression represents accumulated work rather than necessarily elapsed time, since batches and other tasks may execute concurrently. Aggregate processor-hours, QPU execution time, queueing time, and observed end-to-end wall-clock time should be reported separately.

The principal benchmark metrics are summarized below.

| Metric | Benchmark Significance |
| --- | --- |
| Active-space size and qubits | Physical electronic representation and hardware footprint |
| Circuit depth and gate counts | Quantum state-preparation resources |
| Quantum measurement shots | Configuration-discovery cost |
| Unique and recovered configurations | Efficiency of sampling and recovery |
| Number and size of batches | Classical selected-space workload |
| Energy error and fidelity | Quality of the reconstructed electronic state |
| Recovery and eigensolver convergence | Numerical stability of the approximation |
| Classical runtime and peak memory | Cost of projected Hamiltonian diagonalization |
| End-to-end elapsed time | Matched-accuracy hybrid computational performance |

The computational breaking point may arise from quantum hardware noise or insufficient useful configuration samples, but it can also arise from a selected subspace too large for available classical memory or eigensolver runtime. Additional limitations include spin contamination, poorly adapted orbital bases, and recovery schemes that fail to preserve important configurations. No universal active-space or circuit-size threshold can be assigned without specifying the molecular Hamiltonian, sampling distribution, computational hardware, and target accuracy.

## Comparative Evaluation and Crossing-Point Analysis

SQD should be compared with competitive classical electronic-structure solvers and related quantum methods under matched Hamiltonians, symmetry sectors, orbital bases, accuracy criteria, and computational accounting. Its conceptual advantage over direct quantum energy estimation is not a sufficient performance metric because configuration recovery and classical diagonalization can consume substantial resources.

| Method | Computational Paradigm | Representation | Principal Limitation |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Full configuration interaction | Classical | Complete determinant space | Combinatorial growth |
| Selected CI / HCI | Classical | Classically selected determinants | Configuration selection and extrapolation |
| DMRG | Classical | Tensor-network wavefunction | Bond dimension and orbital entanglement |
| VQE / ADAPT-VQE | Hybrid quantum–classical | Optimized quantum circuit | Energy/gradient measurements and optimization |
| SQD | Hybrid quantum–classical | Quantum-sampled determinant subspaces | Sampling support and classical eigensolvers |
| SKQD | Hybrid quantum–classical | Determinants sampled from Krylov-evolved states | Hamiltonian time evolution and configuration discovery |
| TrimSQD | Hybrid quantum–classical | Trimmed and combined selected determinant spaces | Selection accuracy and large-scale diagonalization |

### Comparisons with Classical Selected CI and Variational Quantum Methods

The closest mathematical comparison is with classical selected configuration interaction. Both approaches construct a wavefunction $$\lvert\Psi\rangle=\sum_{I\in\mathcal{S}}c_I\lvert D_I\rangle$$ and diagonalize the Hamiltonian in that determinant set; they differ primarily in how the set is selected. Classical selected CI uses electronic Hamiltonian connectivity, perturbative criteria, or iterative importance selection, while SQD uses a quantum sampling distribution followed by recovery and batching. A controlled test should use identical Hamiltonians and, where possible, the same classical subspace eigensolver, varying only the determinant-selection procedure. This separates the value of quantum sampling from the performance of the underlying classical diagonalization software.

VQE obtains energies from measured Hamiltonian expectation values of parameterized quantum states and typically repeats those measurements during circuit optimization. SQD obtains energies from classical projected Hamiltonians after quantum configuration sampling, and its classical recovery iterations can reuse the same raw measurement data. This may reduce some direct quantum energy-measurement requirements but shifts computational effort to configuration processing and eigensolution. ADAPT-VQE adds the overhead of measuring operator-pool gradients and repeatedly reoptimizing its circuit, providing another distinct accuracy-versus-resources comparison. These methods should be evaluated at equal physical accuracy, rather than compared solely by the number of quantum circuits or selected configurations.

Sample-based Krylov quantum diagonalization (SKQD) and TrimSQD are related but distinct extensions. SKQD prepares Hamiltonian-evolved states, $$\lvert\phi_k\rangle=e^{-i\hat{H}t_k}\lvert\phi_0\rangle$$, and samples their computational-basis support; this changes the quantum state-generation cost and configuration-discovery process. TrimSQD introduces intermediate trimming and merging of determinant information before larger-scale diagonalization. The protein-scale EWF-TrimSQD workflow additionally includes molecular embedding and should be benchmarked as its own Solution, rather than attributing all its performance to standalone SQD.

### Complete Cost and Crossing-Point Analysis

For a family of molecular Hamiltonians characterized by active-space size $$N$$ and a target energy accuracy $$\varepsilon$$, let $$T_{\mathrm{solve}}(N,\varepsilon)$$ denote complete time-to-solution. SQD's end-to-end cost includes molecular Hamiltonian construction, circuit preparation and execution, quantum sampling, configuration recovery, determinant-space construction, projected Hamiltonian evaluation, classical eigensolution, and convergence analysis. A schematic sequential decomposition is

$$
T_{\mathrm{SQD}}
=
T_{\mathrm{setup}}+T_{\mathrm{QPU}}
+T_{\mathrm{recovery}}+T_{\mathrm{subspace}}
+T_{\mathrm{diagonalization}}+T_{\mathrm{other}},
$$

where overlapping operations should instead be accounted for using actual elapsed wall-clock measurements to avoid conflating execution time with accumulated hardware resource-hours.

An accuracy-matched crossing point against a classical method may be defined by

$$
T_{\mathrm{SQD}}(N^*,\varepsilon)
=
T_{\mathrm{classical}}(N^*,\varepsilon).
$$

The location of such a crossing point depends on the molecular correlation regime, orbital basis, quality of quantum-prepared configuration samples, classical selected-space implementation, target observable, and hardware. Particularly informative scaling studies compare energy error versus selected-space dimension, sampling cost versus active-space size, recovery iterations versus energy convergence, classical eigensolver cost versus determinant count, and full workflow time versus ground-state accuracy or fidelity.

The foundational nitrogen and iron-sulfur experiments demonstrate that quantum-generated configurations can be processed into useful correlated electronic-structure approximations at nontrivial scales. They do not establish that quantum sampling is computationally less expensive than the best classical determinant-selection heuristics at matched accuracy. A credible quantum advantage claim therefore requires the benefit attributable to quantum sampling to be measured independently of classical eigensolver improvements, with all resources reported on a comparable basis.

## Reproducibility

A reproducible SQD benchmark requires enough information to reconstruct both the physical electronic problem and the quantum-classical configuration-selection procedure. The molecular record should identify atomic geometry, charge, electron and spin sectors, orbital basis, active-space dimensions, frozen-core treatment, and the one- and two-electron integrals or a precise procedure for generating them. Reference energies and wavefunctions should be supplied when available, with the degree of convergence of the classical reference reported explicitly.

The quantum record should specify the fermion-to-qubit mapping, spin-orbital order, initial electronic reference, state-preparation ansatz, circuit parameters and their initialization, whether circuit parameters were optimized, qubit layout, compiled circuit depth, one- and two-qubit gate counts, and the backend used. Measurement data should identify shot budgets, execution counts, bitstring frequencies or raw bitstrings, calibration information, and the treatment of gate and readout noise. The distinction between simulated, noisy-simulated, and physical-hardware sampling must be maintained.

Configuration processing must specify the target spin-resolved electron numbers, postselection criteria, probabilistic recovery rule, initialization and update of orbital occupation estimates, number of recovery iterations, convergence thresholds, random seeds, and the number of unique or recovered configurations. Each selected determinant batch should be characterized by its size, selection procedure, sampling weights where applicable, and spin or other symmetry handling. These settings determine the reproducible input to the classical eigensolver and are therefore as important as the quantum circuit specification.

The classical calculation should report the projected Hamiltonian construction method, eigensolver type, convergence tolerances, matrix-vector representation, numerical precision, CPU/GPU hardware, processor and node counts, peak memory, and diagonalization time. Energies and reconstructed wavefunction coefficients should be retained for the selected spaces wherever practical. Useful validation outputs include energy and fidelity versus selected-space dimension, shot-count convergence, recovery-iteration trajectories, energy variance, spin diagnostics, and variation between independent sampling runs.

End-to-end performance reporting should separate quantum execution from queueing, circuit compilation, classical recovery, projected Hamiltonian construction, eigensolution, and other overhead. Where independent batches execute concurrently, both elapsed wall-clock time and aggregate processor usage should be supplied. The exact software versions, random seeds, and workflow scripts should be recorded so that differences between implementations can be distinguished from physical or statistical effects.

The publicly available [Qiskit SQD repository](https://github.com/Qiskit/qiskit-sqd) and [documentation](https://qiskit.github.io/qiskit-sqd/) provide software infrastructure for configuration recovery and selected-space workflows. The source entry also identifies Qiskit, PySCF, `ffsim`, and DICE as relevant components of the broader experimental software environment. Implementations that differ in selection, recovery, spin adaptation, or diagonalization should be treated as distinct numerical configurations when their resource requirements or accuracy are compared.
