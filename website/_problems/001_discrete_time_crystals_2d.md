## Scientific Question

Discrete time crystals (DTCs) are nonequilibrium phases of matter characterized by the spontaneous breaking of discrete time-translation symmetry. In periodically driven systems, this symmetry breaking manifests as a robust subharmonic response whose period is an integer multiple of the driving period. Establishing the stability of such behavior in interacting quantum many-body systems is particularly challenging in higher dimensions, where the interplay between disorder, entanglement growth, and thermalization can substantially alter the long-time dynamics.

This benchmark investigates the emergence and persistence of discrete time-crystalline order in two-dimensional spin systems governed by periodically kicked, disordered XXZ Heisenberg interactions. Unlike predominantly Ising-type descriptions, the anisotropic Heisenberg model includes transverse spin-flip interactions that allow additional channels for correlation propagation and many-body relaxation. The central question is whether robust subharmonic oscillations and spatial correlations survive these interactions, and how the resulting dynamical behavior depends on the driving parameters, lattice size, initial state, and evolution time.

The model supports the investigation of localized spin-glass-like behavior, time-crystalline order, and ergodic dynamics within a common physical framework. Distinguishing these regimes requires simultaneous consideration of local spin memory, spatial correlations, and the response at half the driving frequency. Because the calculations involve finite lattices and finite evolution times, the benchmark characterizes observable signatures of time-crystalline behavior without assuming that finite-time oscillations alone establish an indefinitely stable thermodynamic phase.

The computational challenge is to reproduce nonequilibrium many-body dynamics on two-dimensional lattices containing up to 144 interacting spins over tens of Floquet cycles. These systems are sufficiently large that direct state-vector simulation is generally impractical, while approximate classical methods must contend with the growth of correlations and entanglement. The benchmark therefore provides a common physical problem for comparing classical tensor-network methods, digital quantum simulation, and other approaches to many-body time evolution.

## Physical Model Definition

### Hamiltonian and Floquet Evolution

The system consists of spin-$$1/2$$ degrees of freedom on a finite, two-dimensional decorated heavy-hexagonal lattice. Its dynamics are generated by a periodically applied transverse rotation followed by three layers of disordered nearest-neighbor XXZ interactions. The evolution after $$t$$ Floquet cycles is

$$
\lvert\psi_t\rangle
=
U_F^t\lvert\psi_0\rangle,
$$

where $$\lvert\psi_0\rangle$$ is the initial state and $$U_F$$ is the single-cycle Floquet unitary. The latter is defined by

$$
U_F
=
U_{\mathrm{XXZ}}^{(3)}
U_{\mathrm{XXZ}}^{(2)}
U_{\mathrm{XXZ}}^{(1)}
U_X.
$$

The transverse kick acts on every spin through

$$
U_X
=
\prod_{i=1}^{N}
e^{-i\phi X_i},
$$

where $$\phi$$ controls the rotation angle and $$X_i$$ is the Pauli-X operator acting on site $$i$$. Each interaction layer consists of nonoverlapping two-site gates,

$$
U_{\mathrm{XXZ}}^{(k)}
=
\prod_{(i,j)\in G_k}
U_{ij},
$$

with

$$
U_{ij}
=
\exp\left[
-iJ_{ij}
\left(
\epsilon X_iX_j
+
\epsilon Y_iY_j
+
Z_iZ_j
\right)
\right].
$$

Here, $$X_i$$, $$Y_i$$, and $$Z_i$$ denote the Pauli operators at lattice site $$i$$, while $$G_k$$ contains the nearest-neighbor pairs acted upon in interaction layer $$k$$. The longitudinal coupling is normalized relative to the transverse spin-flip terms through the anisotropy parameter $$\epsilon$$. Spatial disorder enters through the interaction strengths,

$$
J_{ij}=1+\delta_{ij},
\qquad
\delta_{ij}\sim\mathcal{U}(-0.5,0.5),
$$

where $$\mathcal{U}$$ denotes the uniform distribution.

The ideal Floquet model evolves unitarily and does not contain intrinsic dissipation. Noise and decoherence arise when the circuit is implemented on imperfect quantum hardware and must be distinguished from the dynamics of the specified model. In particular, agreement with noise-processed experimental results and agreement with ideal unitary evolution are separate validation questions.

### Degrees of Freedom and Lattice Geometry

Each spin has a local Hilbert-space dimension of two, giving a total Hilbert-space dimension

$$
\dim(\mathcal{H})=2^N.
$$

The exponential dependence on the number of spins makes direct classical state-vector simulation prohibitive at the largest benchmark sizes. The geometry nevertheless retains local connectivity, which can be exploited by approximate tensor-network representations and by quantum processors with compatible coupling graphs.

The published work considers three decorated heavy-hexagonal lattice configurations:

| Lattice Configuration | Number of Spins |
| --- | ---: |
| 2 × 2 heavy hexagons | 35 |
| 3 × 3 heavy hexagons | 68 |
| 3 × 7 heavy hexagons | 144 |

These finite lattices have open boundaries and nearest-neighbor interactions determined by their connectivity. Their sizes allow the numerical investigation of spatial correlations and dynamical behavior while introducing finite-size effects that must be considered when interpreting signatures of time-crystalline order.

### Interactions and Physical Regimes

The XXZ interaction contains longitudinal $$Z_iZ_j$$ terms and transverse $$X_iX_j+Y_iY_j$$ terms. The longitudinal contribution favors correlations in the computational basis, whereas the transverse terms permit spin exchange and contribute to the development of many-body entanglement. Disorder in the interaction strengths further modifies the dynamics and can inhibit relaxation, producing regimes in which information about the initial spin configuration persists over experimentally accessible times.

The benchmark investigates three characteristic dynamical regimes. In the localized spin-glass-like regime, the system retains substantial memory of its initial configuration without developing the characteristic robust subharmonic response. The discrete time-crystalline regime exhibits persistent period-doubled oscillations accompanied by spatial correlations, while the ergodic regime is characterized by relaxation and the loss of long-lived spin memory. Their identification depends on the time evolution of the observables defined below rather than on a single instantaneous measurement.

The XXZ interaction possesses axial spin-rotation symmetry before the transverse kick is applied. The complete Floquet unitary has a different symmetry structure, and conservation laws associated with the undriven interaction should not automatically be attributed to the full driven model. Discrete time translation by one Floquet cycle provides the symmetry whose breaking is investigated through the subharmonic response.

## Target Property

The primary target is the nonequilibrium evolution of spin observables that characterize memory, spatial ordering, and discrete time-crystalline behavior. The benchmark emphasizes time-resolved correlations rather than only late-time expectation values, since the distinction between a transient oscillation and a persistent subharmonic response depends on the full dynamical history.

### Spin Autocorrelation and Spatial Order

For an initial computational-basis product state with local spin values $$s_i\in\{-1,1\}$$, define the spin-memory autocorrelation as

$$
\Delta(t)
=
\frac{1}{N}
\sum_{i=1}^{N}
\langle\psi_0\rvert
Z_i(0)Z_i(t)
\lvert\psi_0\rangle.
$$

Because the initial state is an eigenstate of each $$Z_i$$, this expression is equivalent to

$$
\Delta(t)
=
\frac{1}{N}
\sum_{i=1}^{N}
s_i
\langle\psi_t\rvert Z_i\lvert\psi_t\rangle.
$$

The autocorrelation measures the persistence of the initial spin configuration and provides a direct diagnostic of subharmonic oscillations. In a time-crystalline regime, its response exhibits a pronounced component at half the driving frequency. The oscillation amplitude, decay, and sensitivity to model parameters provide quantitative measures of the finite-time stability of the response.

Spatial ordering is characterized by the squared nearest-neighbor spin correlations,

$$
\chi(t)
=
\frac{1}{M}
\sum_{\langle i,j\rangle}
\left[
\langle\psi_t\rvert
Z_iZ_j
\lvert\psi_t\rangle
\right]^2,
$$

where the sum extends over the $$M$$ nearest-neighbor bonds of the lattice. This quantity probes the persistence of local spatial correlations, complementing the information obtained from the spin autocorrelation. Considering both observables helps distinguish time-crystalline dynamics from regimes that retain spin memory without a robust period-doubled response.

### Discrete Time-Crystalline Order

The period-doubled component of the spin dynamics can be isolated using a staggered temporal average,

$$
\Delta_{\mathrm{DTC}}(T)
=
\frac{1}{NT}
\sum_{t=0}^{T-1}
\sum_{i=1}^{N}
(-1)^t s_i
\langle\psi_t\rvert Z_i\lvert\psi_t\rangle.
$$

Here, $$T$$ denotes the number of sampled Floquet cycles. The factor $$(-1)^t$$ selects the response alternating between successive periods of the external drive, while the normalization yields an average over spins and sampled times. The resulting finite-time order parameter can be evaluated across the parameter space to identify regions exhibiting pronounced time-crystalline behavior.

A complementary characterization follows from the discrete Fourier spectrum of $$\Delta(t)$$, particularly the spectral weight at half the driving frequency. The combined use of time-domain and frequency-domain diagnostics is important because finite-duration oscillations may arise from transient dynamics without establishing a stable phase. Their dependence on evolution time, system size, and disorder must therefore be examined.

Additional diagnostics include Hamming-distance distributions, local spin observables, and quantum Fisher information, where appropriate. These quantities provide complementary information about spin-memory loss and the development of many-body correlations, although they are not substitutes for the primary DTC observables.

## Problem Parameters and Conditions

The benchmark is specified by the lattice geometry, XXZ anisotropy, interaction disorder, kick angle, initial spin configuration, and number of Floquet cycles. The published calculations use systems containing 35, 68, and 144 spins, with evolution extending to 50 cycles in the longest reported quantum experiments. Phase-diagram calculations also consider evolution windows of up to 30 cycles.

The principal parameters are summarized below.

| Parameter | Definition | Benchmark Specification |
| --- | --- | --- |
| $$N$$ | Number of spins | 35, 68, 144 |
| $$J_{ij}$$ | Disordered nearest-neighbor coupling | $$1+\delta_{ij}$$ |
| $$\delta_{ij}$$ | Disorder variable | Uniform on $$[-0.5,0.5]$$ |
| $$\epsilon$$ | Relative transverse spin-flip coupling | Varied across parameter scans |
| $$\phi$$ | Transverse kick angle | Varied across parameter scans |
| $$t$$ | Floquet cycle index | Up to 50 cycles |
| $$\lvert\psi_0\rangle$$ | Initial spin configuration | Néel and fully polarized product states |

A representative operating point in the time-crystalline regime is

$$
\epsilon=0.05,
\qquad
\phi=0.45\pi.
$$

These values provide a reference for comparing time evolution and correlation functions between computational methods. Parameter scans in $$\epsilon$$ and $$\phi$$ are used to investigate the transition between localized, time-crystalline, and ergodic behavior.

Initial conditions are computational-basis product states, including Néel-ordered and fully polarized configurations. The finite lattice connectivity and open boundaries must be reproduced when comparing simulations. Disorder realizations should likewise be specified or treated through a documented averaging procedure, since different interaction patterns may produce different finite-size dynamical responses.

No universal numerical target accuracy is imposed. The appropriate tolerance depends on the observable, evolution time, and intended physical conclusion. Comparisons should therefore report deviations from reference results together with the available statistical, numerical, and systematic uncertainties.

## Computational Challenges

The central difficulty is the simulation of long-time quantum dynamics in an interacting two-dimensional system. Although the interactions are local, transverse spin exchange generates correlations that can spread across the lattice during repeated Floquet cycles. The resulting entanglement can increase the computational resources required by classical tensor-network approximations, while exact state-vector methods encounter the exponential growth of the Hilbert space.

Two-dimensional connectivity introduces an additional challenge. Representing the decorated heavy-hexagonal lattice as a one-dimensional tensor network can produce long-range effective connections and increase the necessary bond dimension. Tensor-network approaches that preserve the two-dimensional structure can reduce some of these difficulties, but they require approximate contraction or truncation procedures whose errors may accumulate during time evolution. The accuracy of these approximations must be evaluated for the observables and time windows of interest.

Long-time simulation is especially demanding because the physical signatures are dynamical. Reproducing a few oscillations is insufficient to characterize the robustness of a subharmonic response; the numerical method must maintain controlled accuracy over many cycles while resolving the effects of disorder, interaction anisotropy, and thermalization. In approximate simulations, errors in local expectation values can also affect reconstructed spatial correlations and phase boundaries.

Finite-size and finite-time effects complicate the interpretation of the results. Persistent oscillations over 30 or 50 cycles provide evidence of a long-lived dynamical response but do not, by themselves, establish stability at infinite time or in the thermodynamic limit. A meaningful benchmark must therefore distinguish the accuracy of finite-system predictions from claims about asymptotic phase stability.

For digital quantum simulations, the dominant limitations include gate errors, decoherence, readout noise, and the accumulation of errors across repeated Floquet layers. These limitations introduce a different error structure from tensor-network truncation. Comparing the two approaches requires explicit accounting of their respective approximations rather than assuming that either provides an exact representation of the ideal dynamics.

## Accuracy and Validation Criteria

The primary validation criterion is agreement with reliable reference results for the specified Hamiltonian, lattice, initial state, and evolution time. Accuracy should be assessed through the complete time dependence of $$\Delta(t)$$ and $$\chi(t)$$, together with the finite-time DTC order parameter and the subharmonic Fourier response. A method that reproduces the existence of period doubling but substantially misestimates its amplitude or decay should not be considered quantitatively equivalent to a more accurate simulation.

For a reference observable $$O_{\mathrm{ref}}(t)$$ and a computational estimate $$O_{\mathrm{method}}(t)$$, the pointwise absolute error is

$$
\delta O(t)
=
\left\lvert
O_{\mathrm{method}}(t)
-
O_{\mathrm{ref}}(t)
\right\rvert.
$$

For comparisons over $$T$$ sampled cycles, a useful aggregate metric is the root-mean-square deviation,

$$
\mathrm{RMSE}_O
=
\sqrt{
\frac{1}{T}
\sum_{t=0}^{T-1}
\left[
O_{\mathrm{method}}(t)
-
O_{\mathrm{ref}}(t)
\right]^2
}.
$$

These measures should be reported separately for local spin memory, spatial correlations, and derived order parameters when reference values are available. Agreement in one observable does not guarantee comparable accuracy in the others, particularly when the dynamics involves competing localization and thermalization processes.

Validation should also consider the dependence on system size, initial state, disorder realization, and driving parameters. Near a dynamical crossover, small differences in numerical approximation or hardware noise can affect the apparent phase boundary. Statistical uncertainties from finite measurements and disorder averaging should be distinguished from systematic errors arising from approximate classical evolution or noise-recovery procedures.

For quantum-hardware experiments, raw measurements and noise-recovered estimates should be documented separately. The latter may provide improved estimates of ideal unitary observables, but their reliability depends on the assumptions and calibration data used in the correction. The strongest validation combines comparisons with reliable classical calculations on accessible system sizes, checks against limiting cases, and consistency across independent experimental or numerical procedures.

## Comparative Solutions and Performance

The benchmark admits both classical tensor-network approaches and digital quantum simulation. These methods address the same finite-system dynamics but use substantially different representations and computational resources. Their performance should be compared at fixed model parameters, initial conditions, evolution times, and target observable accuracy.

### Representative Solution Methods

| Solution | Computational Paradigm | Primary Approach | Principal Limitation |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Matrix Product States | Classical computational | One-dimensional tensor-network representation and time evolution | Bond-dimension growth and overhead from two-dimensional connectivity |
| Two-Dimensional Tensor Networks | Classical computational | Belief-propagation-assisted contraction and time evolution | Approximate contraction, truncation, and correlation growth |
| Digital Quantum Simulation | Quantum computational | Direct implementation of repeated Floquet circuits | Gate errors, decoherence, and measurement overhead |

Matrix product state methods provide useful classical reference calculations when the entanglement structure remains tractable, but mapping the two-dimensional connectivity into a one-dimensional representation can substantially increase computational cost. Two-dimensional tensor-network approaches more directly preserve the lattice structure and may extend the accessible system sizes or times, although their accuracy depends on the approximation used to represent and contract the network.

The primary publication employs dynamic belief-propagation-assisted tensor-network methods alongside digital quantum simulation. The digital implementation uses superconducting quantum hardware, including a 144-qubit subset of the IBM `ibm_fez` processor. The longest reported circuits reach 50 Floquet cycles and a two-qubit gate depth of 450. These results demonstrate access to a demanding regime of interacting two-dimensional spin dynamics, but they do not establish a universal computational advantage over optimized classical methods.

### Performance Metrics

For classical simulations, the principal metrics are wall-clock runtime, peak memory, tensor-network bond dimension, contraction cost, and observable error as functions of spin count and Floquet cycles. Approximation parameters should be reported together with convergence tests, since computational savings obtained through aggressive truncation are meaningful only when the target observables remain sufficiently accurate.

For digital quantum simulations, the relevant metrics include physical qubit count, hardware connectivity, circuit depth, one- and two-qubit gate counts, measurement shots, execution time, and the effects of noise and error mitigation. The accuracy of raw and corrected observables should be evaluated separately. The computational resources required for calibration, classical postprocessing, and reconstruction of dynamical observables are part of the overall solution cost.

A consistent time-to-solution comparison requires matching the physical problem and target accuracy. For a system of $$N$$ spins evolved over $$T$$ cycles, this may be expressed as

$$
T_{\mathrm{solve}}
=
T_{\mathrm{solve}}
\left(
N,T,\varepsilon_O
\right),
$$

where $$\varepsilon_O$$ denotes the allowed error in the specified observable or collection of observables. Such a comparison must distinguish the computational workload from elapsed wall-clock time when calculations are parallelized.

### Crossing-Point Analysis

The benchmark is suitable for studying the computational crossing point between classical approximation methods and digital quantum simulation. The relevant regime is determined jointly by lattice size, evolution time, physical parameters, and required accuracy. In particular, the growth of entanglement may increase classical tensor-network costs with evolution time, while quantum hardware costs and errors may grow with circuit depth and measurement requirements.

A meaningful crossing point is reached when competing approaches reproduce the same target observables within matched uncertainties and one approach requires fewer total computational resources. Establishing such a point requires measured performance data or clearly identified extrapolations. The existing results demonstrate the feasibility of studying two-dimensional discrete time-crystalline dynamics with both classical and quantum methods, while leaving a general performance advantage dependent on the physical regime and computational implementation.
